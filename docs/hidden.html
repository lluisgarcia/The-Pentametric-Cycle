The hidden origin

The Fibonacci spiral starts abruptly, at a corner of its first square. Run the rule that draws it backwards from that corner, with squares of half the size each time, and the spiral leads in smoothly from an exact point, H′ = (7/10, −1/10). The point is hidden: it is not in the unit square but in the square next to it, where it is the mirror image of Holden's crossing, one of that square's eight origins. From the note The Hidden Origin: halving squares and the start of the Fibonacci spiral.

1 · Running the spiral backwards

Fibonacci spiralhalving squarescircle of origins of the next square

Before 1, 1 the Fibonacci sides go 0, 1, −1, …: a square of side 0 ends the spiral at the corner E. To go on, the same rule draws a square of side ½ against the right side of the unit square, then ¼, ⅛, … Each arc meets the next with the same tangent, so there is no corner anywhere; only the radius changes, as at every Fibonacci junction.

Each step back is a quarter turn and a halving about one point, so the ends close in on it geometrically: H′ = (7/10, −1/10), exactly. The whole lead-in, all the halving arcs together, is π/2 long: the length of the spiral's own first arc. proved

H′ is the mirror image of Holden's crossing H₀ = (3/10, −1/10) in the side shared with the next square. It lies on that square's circle of origins, so it is one of its eight origins, the origin of the mirror-image tiling that starts there, and it also lies on the circle through the four corners of the unit square. proved

2 · One turn, one halving: the centres and two lines

One move does it all: turn a quarter turn clockwise about H′ and halve, z ↦ H′ − (i/2)(z − H′). It carries the unit square, with its arc, onto the first halving square, and each halving square onto the next. So the unit square and the halving squares are one geometric sequence, with H′ as its centre.

As for any such sequence, the centres alternate between two perpendicular lines through the centre. Here they have slopes 7 and −1/7, and the second one, x + 7y = 0, passes through O, the centre of the unit square. Holden's lines, for the Fibonacci squares themselves, have slopes 3 and −1/3. proved

Every centre is s/√2 from H′, half the diagonal of its square: H′ lies on the circle through the corners of every square of the sequence, the unit square included.

In Gaussian integers: Holden's direction 1 + 3i = (1 + i)(2 + i); the new direction 1 + 7i = (1 + i)(4 + 3i), the diagonal turned by the angle of the 3-4-5 triangle, arctan ¾ = 36.87°.

3 · Any ratio: where does it end?

all the limits, 0 < q < 1Holden's line through O

With squares shrinking by any ratio q, the spiral ends at the corner E turned clockwise about O by 2·arctan q: always on the circle through the corners of the unit square, which was known (Silveira 2013). proved

That circle meets the next square's circle of origins in just two points, and so only two ratios end at an origin: q = ½ (H′, a turn of 53.13°, the mirror of H₀) and q = ⅓ ((7/10, 1/10), a turn of 36.87°, the half turn of H₀ about the middle of the shared side). The two angles are the acute angles of the 3-4-5 triangle that separate the origins. The golden ratio, q = 1/φ, ends at the classical golden pole, √5·H₀, on Holden's line through O. proved

What is known and what is new

Squares shrinking by a constant ratio and their pole are classical (for the golden rectangle, the "eye" of the golden spiral). C. Silveira (2013, arXiv:1308.5941) proved, for any ratio, that the pole lies on the circle through the corners of every square and of the first square, and gave its position; for ratio 2 his formula gives H′. Hoggatt and Alladi (The Fibonacci Quarterly 14, 1976) showed for in-winding golden squares that the centres lie on two perpendicular lines through the pole.

We have not found in the literature: the reading of H′ as an origin of the next square, the mirror image of Holden's crossing; the ratio ⅓ and the half turn; the lines of slopes 7 and −1/7 through O; or the golden pole on Holden's line. The idea of continuing the spiral by halving, and the observation that it ends at the origin of the neighbouring mirror square, are the author's, found in the Spiral Workbench, where the halving lead-in is drawn for all eight spirals and H′ is a centre to turn them about.
