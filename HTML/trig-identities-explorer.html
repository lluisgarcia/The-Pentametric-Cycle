Why this has to be true

Take any right triangle with legs 1 and x. The two non‑right angles, θ and φ, share whatever is left of 180° after the right angle takes 90° — so θ + φ = 90°, always. They're complements by construction.

For complementary angles, one's cosine is the other's sine: cos(θ) = sin(90° − θ) = sin(φ). Swap in θ = arctan(x) and φ = arctan(1/x) and you get the identity at the top.

Reading it off the triangle

From θ's corner, the side of length 1 is adjacent and the side of length x is opposite — so cos θ = 1/√(1+x²).

Stand at φ's corner instead and the same two sides trade roles: the side of length 1 is now opposite, so sin φ = 1/√(1+x²) — the identical ratio. Only the corner you measure from changed.

Why the name fits literally

Picture a unit circle and a vertical line that just grazes its right edge at point T — that line is tangent to the circle, touching it at exactly one point.

Send a ray out from the center O at angle θ and keep extending it past the circle until it reaches that vertical line, at point P. The stretch from T up to P is a real, measurable length — and it's exactly tan θ.

Why that segment equals tan θ

Drop a perpendicular from Q (the point where the ray crosses the circle) down to the axis, at foot F. Triangle O‑F‑Q has legs cos θ and sin θ, and it's similar to triangle O‑T‑P — same angle θ at O, both with a right angle.

Similar triangles keep their ratios: TP / OT = QF / OF. Since OT = 1, that's just TP = sin θ / cos θ = tan θ — a ratio, turned into a length you can point at.
